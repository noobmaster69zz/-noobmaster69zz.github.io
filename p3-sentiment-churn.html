Customer Sentiment & Churn Pipeline

A three-stage pipeline that cleans messy customer reviews, scores them with a language model, flags the customers who sound like they are leaving, and serves it all as a dashboard a non-analyst can read unaided.

01 — Project scope & client's problem

Thousands of reviews, and no way to tell which ones mattered.

An online retailer collects feedback through four channels — website, mobile app, email surveys and the call centre. The volume is useful in principle and unusable in practice: nobody reads every review, and a star rating compresses a paragraph of frustration into a single digit. The customers quietly about to leave rarely announce it in a way a rating captures.

The scope was a pipeline turning that raw feed into two answers a manager can act on: which product areas are making customers unhappy, and which customers are showing signs of leaving — readable by someone with no analytics background and no knowledge of how the pipeline works.

02 — My role & solution

Two signals, deliberately kept independent.

I built all three stages. Ingestion handles the unglamorous work — de-duplicating repeated submissions, dropping blank comments, normalising five inconsistent date formats — before anything downstream sees the data. Enrichment then applies two separate signals: DistilBERT scores each review from −1.0 to +1.0, while a rule-based matcher flags churn language against 17 fixed phrases.

Keeping those signals independent was the key decision. The churn flag is fully explainable — every flag exposes the phrase that triggered it, so nobody is asked to trust a black box — while the sentiment score catches tone the rules would miss. When the two disagree, that disagreement is itself worth a human read.

For the dashboard I treated readability as an engineering requirement rather than decoration. The colour system is a teal-to-rust diverging scale, chosen because the conventional green-red pair collapses into a single hue for people with red-green colour blindness — roughly 8% of men — which would make a sentiment-coded dashboard unusable for them.

03 — My work process

Three separable stages, each verifiable on its own.

01
Ingest500 raw reviews from four channels, carrying the usual defects of a real feed — duplicates, blanks, five date formats.
02
Clean434 usable records survive. The 13% loss rate is measured and reported on the dashboard, not hidden.
03
FlagEach review matched against 17 churn phrases, with matches stored alongside the record so every flag stays traceable.
04
ScoreDistilBERT assigns sentiment from −1.0 to +1.0, independent of both the star rating and the churn rules.
05
StoreEnriched records land in a SQLite warehouse, giving the dashboard one queryable source instead of a chain of files.
06
ServeStreamlit renders KPIs, category sentiment, churn proportions and a searchable record explorer with CSV export.

04 — Outcome & results achieved

A dashboard that explains itself to a first-time visitor.

The application surfaces three headline numbers, automatically names the product category with the unhappiest customers and the one with the largest churn problem, and filters by category, mood, channel, churn status and star rating — with a chip row showing exactly which filters are active, so a narrowed view never masquerades as the whole picture. Missing database, empty table and empty filter each return a plain instruction rather than a stack trace.

Limitations & what I would improve

The dataset is synthetic, generated for demonstration. The pipeline mechanics are real and would run unchanged on production data, but the findings describe generated reviews and are not market insight. The application was deployed to Streamlit Community Cloud during development and is not currently hosted.

Phrase matching buys explainability at the cost of recall. As the demo above shows, a customer who writes "I don't think I'll bother again" signals departure without using any listed phrase, and the rules miss it. A trained classifier would catch more, trading transparency for coverage and requiring a labelled dataset. DistilBERT is also a general-purpose model applied to retail language without fine-tuning.
